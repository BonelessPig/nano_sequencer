The whole board

Signals only. Every chip also takes 5 V and ground from the Nano, which is powered over USB.

Arduino Nano

Seen from above with the USB socket at the top. Labels inside the outline are the ones printed on the board, with the ATmega328P pin name beside the ones the firmware uses.

74HC165 shift register (eight of them)

Chip k of the chain, counting from 0 at the chip nearest the Nano. It reads the four switches of step 2k on inputs H to E and those of step 2k+1 on D to A, the higher letter being the more significant bit. A switch that is on must read high.

MCP4822 DAC

Channel A only, at a gain of 2 from the internal 2.048 V reference, so one step is 1 mV. With LDAC tied to ground the output changes as chip select rises.

Clock and reset inputs, sync switch and run button

Four inputs, each with the chip's internal pull-up switched on, so each pin rests high and something outside pulls it low. The firmware looks at all four once a millisecond.

  • Clock input. Used only while the sync switch is on. A rising edge at the jack is a falling edge at the pin, which Timer/Counter1's input capture flag latches however short the pulse; the firmware reads and clears the flag on its next tick. No interrupt is used. With the tempo pot in the middle a step begins on every edge; turned down it takes every second, third or fourth edge, and turned up it fits two, three or four steps to an edge.
  • Reset input. A rising edge at the jack sends the pattern back to its first step, which plays when the next step is due. The falling edge at the pin is latched in the INT0 flag and read the same way, with the interrupt left off. Ignored while the pattern is stopped.
  • Sync switch. Off, the pattern runs from the internal clock at the tempo the pot sets and the clock input is ignored. On, steps follow the clock input, and with no clock nothing plays.
  • Run button. Each press stops the pattern or starts it again from its first step. The board powers on running.

The switch and the button need no resistor and no capacitor: the pull-ups are inside the chip, and the firmware waits for a contact to hold still for 20 ms before it believes it.

Input stage for the two jacks: suggested, not built

The same circuit twice, one for the clock input and one for the reset input. It has not been built or tested. What the firmware does rely on is that the stage inverts: the pin is low while the jack is high.

  • An NPN transistor of the 2N3904 or BC547 class: emitter to ground, collector to the pin (D8 for the clock, D2 for the reset).
  • The collector is pulled up by the chip's internal pull-up, which the firmware switches on. An external 10 kΩ from the pin to 5 V is an option if a firmer high level or a faster rise is wanted.
  • The base is fed from the jack tip through 100 kΩ, which limits the current into it.
  • A small-signal diode (1N4148) from ground to the base, its cathode at the base, clamps a negative voltage at the jack so the base is never driven far below ground.
  • The jack sleeve goes to ground.

Check before wiring

  • The pin numbers here have been checked against Texas Instruments' SN74HC165 datasheet (SCLS116H, the D, N, NS and PW packages), Microchip's MCP4802/4812/4822 datasheet (DS20002249B, the PDIP, SOIC and MSOP packages) and Arduino's Nano pinout sheet. A part from another maker, or in another package, is worth a look at its own.
  • How the panel switches are wired is not recorded anywhere in the repo. The firmware only needs an input to be high when its switch is on, and a 74HC165 input must never float: a switch to 5 V with a pull-down resistor on each input does both.
  • The tempo pot's value is not recorded either. Its ends go to 5 V and ground because the ADC's reference is AVcc.
  • Only the DAC's 100 nF decoupling capacitor is written down. One across the supply pins of each 74HC165 is normal practice.
  • The 1 kΩ in the MISO line lets an ISP programmer, which uses D11 to D13, win against the chain's output. The serial bootloader does not need it.
  • The input stage for the clock and reset jacks is a suggestion that has not been built or tested: its part values, the level at which it switches and how it stands up to the voltages a modular system puts out are all still to be found on the bench. Do not wire a jack straight to D8 or D2.
  • That the two edge flags latch with their interrupts off is taken from the ATmega328P datasheet and from the emulated board, whose model of it is this project's own. It has not been seen on a real chip yet.
  • The sync switch and the run button go from their pins to ground with nothing else: no resistor, and no connection to 5 V.
  • Do not patch the gate, clock or pitch CV into other equipment until their buffer stages are built: nothing protects those pins (ARCHITECTURE.md, open items 9 and 10).
  • A1 (PC1) is configured as an output although nothing uses it. Leave it unconnected.
