Panel

The light on each step is the board's own step LED, as the chip last latched it into the two 74HC595s: lit for the first half of a step, and dark while stopped and in calibration mode. The outline round a step is the step counted here, which the labels are worked out from; see under the input jacks.

Mode switches 2 to 0 choose the engine, as a number:

The Klee engine sends a pattern of bits round a register of sixteen stages, one stage a step. Each step is a stage: its highest switch (8) is its pattern switch and its low three (4, 2 and 1) its value, 0 to 7. The note is the sum of the values under the active stages, 15 at most, and a sum of 0 is a rest. The pattern switches only count at a load, which copies them into the register: on the first step, and on the step after mode switch 3 is moved either way. The values are read on every step. The step lights show the register itself: every active stage is lit, and the pattern can be seen going round, turned over by invert and drifting with chance. That comes from the chip and is not worked out here, where the random numbers are not followed. What is counted here is only how far the register has moved since it was loaded, 0 to 15 and round again, given beside the mode switches; no step is outlined, since none is being played.

The chance engine plays the sixteen steps as the plain engine does, but skips some, which are rests, and with shuffle on picks each step at random. A shuffled step cannot be worked out here: the debug image's log names it and it is outlined from that; the release image has no log, so no step is outlined while shuffle is on. The step lights still show it on both, since they are the chip's own.

The Cartesian engine plays the step at column x of row y of this grid. Both start at 0 and go round from 3 to 0, each moving on every so many steps as the mode switches say. With switches 6 to 3 all off that is the sixteen steps in order; other settings trace other paths, some of which never reach every step.

The Euclidean engine plays the sixteen steps in order, but a step sounds only on a beat; any other is a rest. The beats are spread as evenly as a bar of sixteen allows, the first on step 0. The steps marked "beat" are the beats at the present setting; one of them is still a rest if its own switches are all off.

The stage engine plays eight stages. Steps 0 to 7 are the stages' notes and steps 8 to 15 their controls, step 8 for stage 0 and so on. A control's low two switches (2 and 1) are the stage's length, 1 to 4 steps; its high two (8 and 4) are the gate mode: single plays the note on the stage's first step only, repeat on every step, hold once for the whole stage, and rest not at all. The stage counted as playing is outlined with its control, which counts its steps; the light is on the stage's note.

Sets steps 12 to 15 to 10, 5, 10, 5 and power cycles the board, which is how the mode is entered.

Input jacks

Sends the pattern back to its first step, which plays when the next step is due. Ignored while stopped.

The clock input is followed only with the sync switch on: a step then begins on its edges, the tempo pot sets how many edges to a step or steps to an edge, and with no edges nothing plays. Each press of the run button stops the pattern or starts it again from the first step. A power cycle leaves the sync switch and the clock source as they are, and the board comes up running.

The step lights on the panel are the chip's own output and are only passed on. The step outlined is another thing: it is counted here from the clock output, starting again at 0 after a start or a reset made here, and after a change of engine. The debug image also names each step in its log, and that is believed over the count. The release image has no log, so there the count is all there is, and a reset that lands within a tick or two of a step can leave it out. A switch is counted from the panel read at which the chip took it, so it does not. Where the outline and the light disagree, the light is right. On the stage engine the log names the stage, and which of its steps is playing is always counted, from the lengths on the panel as they stood when each stage began. On the Klee engine what is counted is how far the register has moved since its last load, and the count goes back to 0 when mode switch 3 is moved. On the Cartesian engine the two counters are counted as the firmware counts them, from the mode switches as each step begins. On the chance engine with shuffle on there is nothing to count, and only the log can say which step was picked, or the step lights. Nothing here follows the firmware's random numbers.

The engine is taken from the mode switches as each step begins, and a change of engine starts the new one's pattern from its beginning. Mode switches 3 to 7 belong to the engine chosen, which reads them on every step.

Calibration mode

The sequence is stopped: no steps and no clock. The gate is held high and the pitch CV sits at one point of the calibration table, to be measured.

  • Step 0's switches pick the point, 0 to 4: one per octave.
  • The tempo pot trims its voltage, a millivolt at a time.
  • Step 15's lowest switch, off and on again, stores it. The gate drops for a quarter of a second to show it did.

To leave: change any switch on steps 12 to 15, then power cycle. Keep the EEPROM to play from what was stored.

How to use calibration mode

        
The mode sets the table the firmware plays pitch from: five points,
            one per octave, each the DAC value that should give exactly 0, 1, 2,
            3 and 4 V. On a real board each point is trimmed against a voltmeter
            on the pitch CV output. Here the DAC is ideal, so the Pitch CV figure
            above stands in for the meter and nothing needs correcting, but a
            point can be stored off its mark to see it take effect.

        
    
            
  1. Enter. Click "Enter calibration mode". The sequence stops,
                the gate goes high and stays there, and the pitch CV holds
                still. With the sound on, that is one steady tone.
    2. 
            
  2. Pick a point. Set step 0's switches to 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4
                (the switches marked 1, 2 and 4; a higher value counts as 4).
                Point n should read n volts.
    3. 
            
  3. Trim it. Turn the tempo pot until the Pitch CV reads the
                voltage wanted. The middle of the pot (a reading of about 512)
                gives the point's nominal value, and its travel covers 64 mV
                below that to 63 mV above, a millivolt for every 8 of the
                reading. Point 0 cannot go below 0 V.
    4. 
            
  4. Store it. Switch step 15's lowest switch (marked 1) off
                and on again. The gate drops for a quarter of a second, heard as
                a gap in the tone, and the debug image logs the point and the
                value stored. It is written to the EEPROM there and then.
    5. 
            
  5. Repeat for any other points. A point that is not stored
                keeps the value it had.
    6. 
            
  6. Leave. Change any switch on steps 12 to 15 so they no
                longer read 10, 5, 10, 5, then click "Power cycle" with "Keep
                the EEPROM" ticked. The debug image's first log line then says
                Cal = 1, a stored table was found, and notes are played from it,
                on the straight line between the octaves either side.
    7. 
        

        
To go back to the ideal table, untick "Keep the EEPROM" and power
            cycle: the EEPROM is erased and the first log line says Cal = 0.
